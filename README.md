ETF Investment Bias Checklist. Before buying: “Am I buying because of data or recent excitement?”
* [ ] Am I buying because the ETF has recently gone up sharply?
* [ ] Am I afraid of missing out (FOMO)?
* [ ] Would I still buy if I hadn't seen its recent returns?
* [ ] Have I looked beyond the last few weeks or months?
* [ ] Have I checked longer-term performance and drawdowns?
* [ ] Do I understand what index or assets the ETF tracks?
* [ ] Am I reacting to news headlines rather than my investment plan?
* [ ] Am I comparing this ETF with reasonable alternatives?

Before investing: “What is my maximum acceptable loss or drawdown?”
* [ ] Do I know how much this investment could realistically fall?
* [ ] Have I checked the ETF/index's historical maximum drawdown?
* [ ] Can I emotionally tolerate a 20%, 30%, or 50% fall, depending on the asset?
* [ ] Would I panic and sell if the investment fell sharply?
* [ ] Have I decided beforehand what I will do during a major market fall?
* [ ] Do I have enough diversification?
* [ ] Am I investing money that I won't need in the near future?
* [ ] Is my investment amount appropriate for my risk tolerance?

Investigate each ETF you own using the same questions:
1. What was its worst drawdown? (MDD)
2. How long did it take to recover? (TUW)
3. What happened to someone who kept investing during that period?
4. How volatile were the returns along the way?
5. Can I realistically continue buying during a similar event?
That could actually become a nice investigative framework for every ETF you own.

A simple rule before investing. Ask yourself these five questions:
1. Am I buying because of data or recent excitement?
2. What could prove me wrong?
3. Would I buy this at today's price if I didn't already own it?
4. Am I following a plan or following the crowd?
5. What is my maximum acceptable loss or drawdown?
The goal isn't to eliminate biases completely—we all have them. The goal is to build a process that prevents emotions from making the final decision.

John Bogle's key 10 rules of investing are:
1. Reversion to the mean: What's hot today isn't likely to be hot tomorrow.
2. Time is your friend: Let the miracle of compound interest work for you.
3. Buy right and hold tight: Stick to the plan no matter how greedy or scared you become.
4. Have realistic expectations: You are unlikely to get rich quickly. 
5. Forget the needle, buy the haystack: Buy broad-based index or exchange-traded funds and you can cut stock risk, style risk, and manager risk. 
6. Minimize the “croupier’s” take: Minimize fees by investing in low-cost funds. This increases your return.
7. There’s no escaping risk: There’s no wealth without risk.
8. Don’t fight the last war: What worked in the past is no predictor of what will work in future. 
9. The hedgehog beats the fox: Foxes represent the financial institutions that charge far too much for their artful, complicated advice. A hedgehog does one thing when threatened — he curls up into a spiny ball. Simple, but effective, like an index fund. 
10. Stay the course: The secret to successful investing isn’t forecasting or good stock picking. It is about making a plan, sticking to it, and eliminating unnecessary risks.

11 Boglehead principles:
Live Below Your Means: This is the essential first step. By spending less than you earn, you generate the surplus needed to invest and build wealth.
Develop a Workable Plan: A solid plan is your roadmap to success. This includes setting clear financial goals and writing down your investment strategy to refer back to in times of uncertainty.
Never Bear Too Much or Too Little Risk: Balance is key. Taking on too much risk can lead to panic selling, while taking too little can prevent you from reaching your goals.
Invest Early and Often: This principle emphasizes the power of compounding. The sooner you start and the more regularly you contribute, the greater your long-term growth potential.
Diversify: Don't put all your eggs in one basket. Spreading your investments across different asset classes (like stocks and bonds) helps to manage risk.
Never Try to Time the Market: As you know well, predicting market highs and lows is a fool's errand. The disciplined approach is to stay invested through market cycles.
Use Index Funds When Possible: This is the core of Bogle's strategy. Broad-market index funds offer instant diversification, low costs, and historically reliable returns by simply capturing the entire market's performance.
Keep Costs Low: Seemingly small costs, like expense ratios, compound over time and can drastically eat into your returns. This is why you favor ultra-low-cost ETFs.
Minimize Taxes: Managing the tax impact of your investments is a key aspect of maximizing your net returns, for example, by using tax-advantaged accounts.
Invest with Simplicity: A simple portfolio—perhaps just a few broad-market index funds—is easier to understand, manage, and stay disciplined with than a complex collection of individual stocks and funds.
Stay the Course: This is the final, crucial piece. Once you have a plan, you must have the discipline to stick with it through market volatility and economic uncertainty, resisting the urge to make impulsive changes.

🧭 THE STOIC INVESTOR’S CHECKLIST (For When the Voice Creeps In)
1. PAUSE. BREATHE. OBSERVE. LEARN. ADAPT.
“Between stimulus and response, there is a space. In that space is our power to choose our response.” — Viktor Frankl
□ Stop. Do not act immediately.
□ Breathe. Three deep breaths.
□ Name the voice. Is it fear? Greed? Impatience? FOMO? Pride?
□ Observe it. Don't fight it. Just notice it. Say: “Ah, there you are again.”

2. REMEMBER YOUR SYSTEM.
“The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way.” — Marcus Aurelius
□ Is this an emergency? (No. Emergencies are health, family, survival—not market dips.)
□ Does my system have a rule for this? (Yes. Tranches. Step down/up. Buy on schedule.)
□ Am I following my pre-decided plan? (If yes: stay the course. If no: return to it.)

3. INVERT THE THOUGHT.
“All I want to know is where I’m going to die, so I’ll never go there.” — Charlie Munger
□ What if I do the opposite of what this voice wants? Would I be better off?
□ What would this look like in 5 years? Will this decision matter?
□ What would Warren, Charlie, or Marcus say? (They'd say: stay calm, stick to the system.)

4. CHECK YOUR TEMPERAMENT.
“The stock market is a device for transferring money from the impatient to the patient.” — Warren Buffett
□ Am I bored? (Good. Boredom is a sign of a sound strategy.)
□ Am I excited? (Caution. Excitement leads to mistakes.)
□ Am I anxious? (Return to the system. The system protects you.)
□ Do I need to check my portfolio right now? (No. Check it on your scheduled day.)

5. GROUND YOURSELF IN WHAT YOU KNOW.
“I have no special talent. I am only passionately curious.” — Albert Einstein
□ Do I understand this investment? (If not: it's out. Too hard.)
□ Is this a bet or a strategy? (If it's a bet: size it small. If it's a strategy: stick to it.)
□ Is my expense ratio still low? (Yes. ~0.13%. That’s world-class.)
□ Am I diversified? (Yes. Large, mid, small, US, gold.)

6. REMEMBER YOUR "WHY."
“We become what we think about most of the time.” — Earl Nightingale
□ What's my goal? (₹7.5L by 31 Dec 2026.)
□ Am I on track? (Yes. Systematically accumulating.)
□ Will this decision help or hurt that goal? (If it helps: act. If it hurts: stop.)
□ Why did I start this journey? (Freedom. Peace. A life well-lived.)

7. ASK THE FINAL TWO QUESTIONS.
□ “Is this within my circle of competence?”
Yes? Proceed with curiosity. No? File it in the “too hard” pile and walk away.
□ “Will I thank myself for this decision in 5 years?”
Yes? Do it. No? Don't.

8. ACT, THEN RELEASE.
“You have power over your mind—not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength.” — Marcus Aurelius
□ Make the decision. (Based on the checklist.)
□ Execute. (Calmly. Mechanically.)
□ Accept the outcome. (You can't control the result. You can only control the process.)
□ Let go. (Close the tabs. Go for a walk. Cook a meal. Live your life.)

💬 A Mantra for the Voice. When the voice creeps in, say this to yourself:
“I am not my thoughts. I am my actions. My system is sound. My goal is clear. I am patient. I am prepared. I am becoming what I think about.”

🛠️ How to Strengthen Your Frame of Reference
Here’s your personal checklist to keep your frame sharp:

□ Name your frame. Before making any decision, ask: "Through what lens am I viewing this?"
□ Test your frame. Ask: "Would Warren, Charlie, or Marcus see it the same way?"
□ Expand your frame. Read, walk, listen to opposing views.
□ Check for distortion. Ask: "Am I seeing reality, or am I seeing what I want to see?"
□ Re-center. Return to your core principles: patience, discipline, low-cost, long-term.

💎 The Bottom Line (For Your Walks): When the creeping voice comes back—and it will—it's not trying to mislead you because it's evil. It's trying to mislead you because it's operating from a different frame of reference:
The frame of fear, The frame of greed, The frame of impatience, The frame of comparison.
Your job is not to eliminate that voice. Your job is to recognize its frame, then consciously choose your own.
You are not the voice. You are the one who observes the voice. And your frame—forged by Stoics, investors, and your own disciplined action—is sound. Now, next time you walk, listen to your audio mentors, and remind yourself:
